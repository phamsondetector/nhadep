The Eight Trigram Formation of To Lich River: The Mysterious Suppression of Thang Long’s Dragon Vein for Thousands of Years


To Lich River – the ancient river flowing through the heart of Hanoi – is not only a silent witness to countless historical ups and downs but also carries a mysterious legend that has fascinated generations of Vietnamese people: the Bagua (Eight Trigram) formation for suppressing geomancy created by Gao Pian. In 2001, during river dredging and embankment construction, a series of strange artifacts were unearthed, sending shockwaves through public opinion — from lim-wood piles arranged in bizarre patterns, human skeletons nailed through the shoulders, to large animal bones and ancient ceramics. Could this truly be the “feng shui formation” that Gao Pian established more than 1,200 years ago to block Vietnam’s dragon vein? Or is it merely an exaggerated folk legend?
Let’s explore this captivating and mysterious story together.

Gao Pian – “Feng Shui Master” and Jiedushi of the Tang Dynasty

Gao Pian (821–887), a Chinese general and renowned feng shui practitioner of the Tang Dynasty, was appointed Jiedushi (military governor) of Giao Chau (present-day northern Vietnam) in the 9th century. According to official historical records, Gao Pian played a major role in constructing Dai La Citadel — the precursor to Thang Long (later Hanoi). The citadel measured over 6.6 km in circumference, stood nearly 9 meters high, and featured moats, watchtowers, and a robust defensive system.

However, in Vietnamese folklore, Gao Pian is remembered as a “feng shui enemy.” Legends say he rode a paper kite across the land to observe the dragon veins, then used talismans and formations to suppress and seal them, preventing Vietnam from producing heroes and talents so it would remain a colony of China forever. One of the key “hot spots” he targeted was the To Lich River area, regarded as the “dragon’s tail” of the Thang Long dragon vein.
According to folk tales, while building Dai La Citadel, the To Lich River section kept subsiding. Gao Pian believed this was caused by excessively strong dragon energy, so he created an Eight Trigram formation beneath the riverbed: burying lim-wood piles, human and animal remains arranged according to the eight trigrams of the I Ching (Qian, Kun, Zhen, Xun, Kan, Li, Gen, Dui), combined with metal objects and incantations to lock the spiritual energy, harden the ground, and completely seal the dragon vein.

The Shocking Discovery of 2001: The “Sacred Artifacts” Emerge

In September 2001, Construction Team No. 12 of VIC Construction Company, led by Mr. Nguyen Hung Cuong, was dredging and building embankments along To Lich River at the section passing through An Phu Village, Nghia Do Ward, Cau Giay District (formerly Thon Doai Mon – the western gate of Dai La Citadel).

As excavators dug deeper, they uncovered:
• Dozens of vertical lim-wood piles arranged in short rows forming unusual geometric patterns (many described them as resembling a Bagua diagram).
• Eight sets of human skeletons (some sources say 7–8), several with iron nails driven through the shoulder blades, placed among the wooden piles.
• Teeth and bones of large animals: elephants, horses, and buffalo.
• More than ten glazed ceramic plates, jade-green glazed pottery, bowls, plates, knives, sickles, sewing needles, and ancient round coins with square holes.
• Numerous old bricks and roof tiles from the Ly, Tran, and Le dynasties.
Construction was immediately halted. Scientists, including Professor Tran Quoc Vuong (former Director of the Hanoi Museum), were invited to the site. Prof. Vuong suggested that these could be remnants of a 9th-century Bagua suppression formation designed to protect the gate of Dai La Citadel.
At the same time, a series of strange phenomena occurred: workers fell ill, accidents happened repeatedly, machinery broke down, and rumors spread about “sacred artifacts” causing disturbances. Many believed that disturbing the formation had provoked “yin soldiers” or released the dragon energy.

Fact or Fiction: What Does Science Say?

Despite the vivid legend, subsequent archaeological studies reached different conclusions:
• The artifacts (pottery, ancient coins, bricks) date no earlier than the 11th century (Ly Dynasty) — approximately 200 years later than Gao Pian’s time in the 9th century. There is no evidence linking them to any Tang Dynasty project.
• The wooden pile structure may be the remains of an ancient construction, religious ritual, or even the foundation of houses from the Ly–Tran period, rather than a “suppression formation.”
• Many historians, such as Duong Trung Quoc, suggest the “strange phenomena” resulted from geological factors: the area is the confluence of three rivers with unstable soil layers, combined with psychological effects and feng shui rumors.

In summary, there is no scientific evidence confirming this was Gao Pian’s Eight Trigram formation. Yet the legend remains powerfully alive in folk consciousness and has become part of Hanoi’s intangible cultural heritage.
 

Cultural Significance and Lessons for Today

Whether real or imaginary, the story of the To Lich River suppression reflects a beautiful aspect of Vietnamese culture: the belief in feng shui, dragon veins, and harmony between humans and nature. It also shows how our ancestors used ancient knowledge (the I Ching and Eight Trigrams) to explain and “control” the world around them.
Today, To Lich River has undergone major renovation and become cleaner and more modern. Some of the 2001 artifacts have been preserved, while others were reburied according to spiritual rituals. The story continues to be told through books, documentaries, and online articles, reminding us that history is not just dry facts but also layers of legend that form the soul of a nation.
